## Scene 01 — hook

Almost four hundred years ago, a scientist called Jan van Helmont planted a small willow tree in a pot of dried soil. He weighed the tree. He weighed the soil. Then, for five years, he gave it nothing but water. The tree grew. It gained about seventy-four kilograms. And the soil? It had lost only about fifty-seven grams. So where did a whole tree's worth of mass come from? Not the soil. Most of the wood came out of thin air. By the end of this video, you'll know exactly how, and how to explain it in an exam.

## Scene 03 — definition

You eat to get your food. Plants don't. Plants make their own food, by photosynthesis. Here's the definition the mark scheme wants. Learn it word for word. Photosynthesis is the process by which plants synthesise carbohydrates from raw materials using energy from light. **(pause 1)** Let's break that down. Synthesise means make. The carbohydrate it makes is glucose, a simple sugar. The raw materials are carbon dioxide and water. And the energy to do it comes from light.

## Scene 04 — equation

Now the word equation. Carbon dioxide, plus water, gives glucose, plus oxygen. And it happens in the presence of light and chlorophyll, so we write those on the arrow. They're conditions, not reactants. Light isn't a substance, and chlorophyll isn't used up. So where do the raw materials come from? Carbon dioxide diffuses into the leaf from the air. Water is absorbed from the soil by the roots, and travels up the stem, in the xylem, to the leaf. Oxygen is a waste product. Some is used in the plant's own respiration, and the rest diffuses out of the leaf, into the air.

## Scene 05 — balanced

You also need to know the balanced symbol equation. Six C O two, plus six H two O, gives C six H twelve O six, plus six O two. Let's check it balances. Carbon: six on the left, six on the right. Hydrogen: twelve on each side. Oxygen: twelve plus six makes eighteen on the left, and six plus twelve makes eighteen on the right. It balances.

## Scene 06 — chlorophyll

So, what does the light actually do? Inside the cells of a leaf are chloroplasts. Chloroplasts contain chlorophyll, a green pigment. Chlorophyll absorbs light. Then it transfers energy from light into energy in chemicals, for the synthesis of carbohydrates. In other words, that energy ends up stored in glucose. Be careful with your wording here. Never write that chlorophyll makes energy. Energy can't be made. Chlorophyll transfers it. So use the syllabus word: transfers.

## Scene 07 — uses

So the plant has made glucose. What happens to it? There are five uses to learn. One. Glucose is used in respiration, to release energy for the plant's cells. Two. Glucose is turned into starch, as an energy store. Starch is insoluble, so storing it doesn't affect the movement of water by osmosis. Three. Glucose is used to make cellulose, which builds the cell walls. Four. Glucose is changed into sucrose, another sugar. Sucrose is transported in the phloem, to other parts of the plant. And five. Sugars are used to make nectar, in flowers, to attract insects for pollination. Respiration. Starch. Cellulose. Sucrose. Nectar.

## Scene 08 — minerals

But glucose only contains carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. To grow, a plant also needs proteins, and proteins are made from amino acids. Amino acids contain nitrogen. So plants absorb nitrate ions from the soil, through their roots. Nitrate ions are needed to make amino acids. Without enough nitrate, the plant has stunted growth, and its older leaves turn yellow. Plants also absorb magnesium ions. Magnesium ions are needed to make chlorophyll. Without enough magnesium, the leaves turn yellow between the veins. Less chlorophyll means less light is absorbed, so there's less photosynthesis. In an exam, link three things: the ion, what it's used to make, and what happens without it.

## Scene 09 — leaf

Most photosynthesis happens in the leaves, and leaves are adapted for it. Most leaves are broad and thin. Broad means a large surface area, to absorb more light. Thin means a short distance for carbon dioxide to diffuse to the cells inside. Now let's cut a leaf open. This is a cross-section through the leaf of a dicotyledonous plant. That's a plant whose seeds have two seed leaves. It's a simplified diagram. For each part, you need to know its name, and how it helps photosynthesis. Let's go through it layer by layer, from the top. First, the waxy cuticle. It reduces water loss, and because it's transparent, light passes straight through it. Next, the upper epidermis. These cells are transparent too, and they have no chloroplasts, so light passes through to the cells below. Then the palisade mesophyll. These cells are tall, tightly packed, close to the top surface, and full of chloroplasts. So they absorb the most light. This is where most photosynthesis happens.

## Scene 10 — layers

Below the palisade is the spongy mesophyll. Its cells are rounder, and loosely packed, with air spaces between them. The air spaces let carbon dioxide diffuse to the cells, and they give a large surface area for gas exchange. Running through the leaf are vascular bundles. The xylem brings water and mineral ions into the leaf. The phloem carries sucrose and amino acids away, to the rest of the plant. On the underside is the lower epidermis, with tiny pores called stomata. One pore is called a stoma. Carbon dioxide diffuses in through the stomata, and oxygen diffuses out. Each stoma is opened and closed by a pair of guard cells. In the light, they open the stomata, so carbon dioxide can diffuse in. So, from top to bottom: cuticle, upper epidermis, palisade mesophyll, spongy mesophyll, vascular bundle, lower epidermis with stomata and guard cells.

## Scene 11 — starch-test

So how can you show that a leaf has been photosynthesising? Test it for starch, because a leaf stores some of its glucose as starch. First, destarch the plant. Put it in the dark for a day or two. With no light, it can't photosynthesise, so it uses up its stored starch. That means any starch you find later must have been made during your experiment. Step one: put the leaf in boiling water for about a minute. This kills the cells, and breaks down the cell membranes, so the iodine can get in later. Step two: turn off the Bunsen burner. Then put the leaf in a boiling tube of ethanol, and stand it in the hot water. Ethanol is flammable, so never heat it with a naked flame. The hot ethanol removes the chlorophyll, so the colour change will be easy to see. Step three: dip the leaf in warm water. The ethanol makes it brittle, and the water softens it. Step four: spread the leaf on a white tile, so the colour is easy to see, and add iodine solution. If it turns blue-black, starch is present. If it stays orange-brown, there's no starch.

## Scene 12 — needs

Now you can use that test to show what photosynthesis needs. Each experiment needs a control. A control is identical to the test, except for the one factor you're investigating. It shows that the result was caused by that factor. First, chlorophyll. Take a destarched plant with variegated leaves: leaves that are partly green and partly white. The white parts have no chlorophyll. Leave it in the light for a few hours, then test a leaf. Only the green parts turn blue-black. The white parts stay orange-brown. The green parts are the control. So chlorophyll is needed. Second, light. Take a destarched plant, and cover part of one leaf with black paper. Leave it in the light for a few hours. After the test, only the uncovered part turns blue-black. The uncovered part is the control. So light is needed. Third, carbon dioxide. Take a destarched plant, and seal one leaf, still attached, inside a flask with soda lime, which absorbs carbon dioxide. The control is an identical flask, with no soda lime. After a few hours in the light, only the control leaf turns blue-black. So carbon dioxide is needed.

## Scene 13 — rate-method

Next: how fast is photosynthesis happening? That's its rate. To measure it, use pondweed, a water plant. Put it in water with a little sodium hydrogencarbonate, which supplies carbon dioxide. In the light, the pondweed gives off bubbles of gas, mostly oxygen. Count the bubbles per minute. Or, for more accurate results, collect the gas and measure its volume, because bubbles can be different sizes. To change the light intensity, move a lamp closer or further away. The closer the lamp, the higher the light intensity. So, light intensity is the independent variable: the one you change. The rate is the dependent variable: the one you measure. And everything else must be kept constant: the same plant, the same carbon dioxide concentration, the same temperature, and no other lights on. To keep the temperature steady, stand the beaker in a water bath. An LED lamp helps, because it gives off much less heat than a filament bulb. At each new distance, wait a few minutes before you count, so the plant can adjust. Then repeat, and calculate a mean. You can test the other two factors the same way. Just change one thing at a time. To vary carbon dioxide, use different concentrations of sodium hydrogencarbonate. To vary temperature, set the water bath to different temperatures.

## Scene 14 — rate-results

Here are the results. As light intensity increases, the rate of photosynthesis increases. Then it levels off. Increase the carbon dioxide concentration, and you get the same shape: the rate increases, then levels off. Temperature is different. The rate increases up to an optimum, then falls quickly. That's because the reactions of photosynthesis are catalysed by enzymes. Below the optimum, raising the temperature gives the molecules more kinetic energy, so there are more frequent effective collisions between enzymes and their substrates. Above the optimum, the enzymes are denatured. Their active sites change shape, so the substrates no longer fit. When a question says describe a graph, say what happens, and quote numbers from the axes, with their units. When it says explain, say why.

## Scene 15 — limiting

Now let's explain those shapes, using limiting factors. A limiting factor is something in short supply that limits the rate of a process. Back to the light graph. On this rising part, the rate increases as light intensity increases. So light intensity is the limiting factor. On this flat part, more light makes no difference. Light intensity is no longer the limiting factor. Something else is, like carbon dioxide concentration, or temperature. Now repeat the experiment at a higher carbon dioxide concentration. At low light intensity, the two curves are the same, because light intensity is still the limiting factor. But the new curve keeps rising for longer, and levels off higher. That shows carbon dioxide was the limiting factor on the flat part. In the exam, name the limiting factor, and give the reason: increasing it increases the rate. Different conditions have different limiting factors. At dawn, or on a dull day, light intensity is usually the limiting factor, because there's not much light. On a cold winter's day, it's often temperature. The molecules have less kinetic energy, so there are fewer effective collisions. And on a bright, warm summer's day, there's plenty of light and warmth, so it's often carbon dioxide concentration. There's only a tiny amount of carbon dioxide in the air.

## Scene 16 — predict

One more practical. Hydrogencarbonate indicator shows how much carbon dioxide is in a solution. At the normal level of carbon dioxide in air, it's red. With more carbon dioxide, it turns yellow. With less carbon dioxide, it turns purple. Here are three tubes of indicator. They're sealed with bungs, so no carbon dioxide from the air can get in or out. One has pondweed, in bright light. One has pondweed too, but its tube is wrapped in foil, so it's dark inside. And one has no pondweed at all. What colour will each tube be, after a few hours? Pause the video, and make your prediction. **(pause 4)**

## Scene 17 — reveal

Here's what happens. In the light, the indicator turns purple. Photosynthesis is faster than respiration, so the pondweed takes in more carbon dioxide than it gives out. In the dark, it turns yellow. There's no photosynthesis, but respiration carries on, so the pondweed gives out carbon dioxide. The tube with no pondweed stays red. That's the control. It shows that the pondweed caused the colour changes. And in dim light, the two rates can be equal. Then there's no net uptake or release of carbon dioxide, so the indicator stays red. Which busts a classic myth: that plants only respire at night. Plants respire all the time, day and night, just like you. In bright light, photosynthesis is just faster, so there's a net uptake of carbon dioxide. Net means overall: the plant takes in more carbon dioxide than it gives out. Net is the word examiners look for.

## Scene 18 — resolve

So, back to van Helmont's willow. Where did those seventy-four kilograms come from? Carbon dioxide, from the air, and water, from the soil. Joined into glucose, using energy from light, then built into cellulose, starch and proteins. The little that the soil lost was mostly mineral ions, like nitrate and magnesium. Van Helmont thought his tree was made of water. He was only partly right. A living tree is full of water. But most of the wood itself was built from carbon dioxide, taken out of thin air.

## Scene 19 — recap

Let's recap. Photosynthesis: carbon dioxide plus water gives glucose plus oxygen, in the presence of light and chlorophyll. Chlorophyll transfers energy from light into energy in chemicals. Glucose is used for respiration, starch, cellulose, sucrose and nectar. Nitrate ions are needed to make amino acids. Magnesium ions are needed to make chlorophyll. Leaves are broad, thin, and built in layers, each adapted for photosynthesis. Light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration and temperature can all be limiting factors. And that's plant nutrition.
