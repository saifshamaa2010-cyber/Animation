## Scene 01 — hook

Almost four hundred years ago, a scientist called Jan van Helmont planted a small willow tree in a pot of dry soil. He weighed the tree. He weighed the soil. Then, for five years, he gave it nothing but water. The tree grew. It gained about seventy-four kilograms. And the soil? It had lost about fifty-seven grams. So where did a whole tree's worth of mass come from? Not the soil. Most of it came out of thin air.

## Scene 03 — definition

You eat to get your food. Plants don't. They make their own, by photosynthesis. Here's the definition the mark scheme wants. Photosynthesis is the process by which plants synthesise carbohydrates from raw materials using energy from light. Synthesise just means make. The carbohydrate they make first is glucose, a sugar. The raw materials are carbon dioxide and water. And the energy comes from light.

## Scene 04 — equation

Here's the word equation. Carbon dioxide, plus water, gives glucose, plus oxygen. Light and chlorophyll go above the arrow. They're conditions, not reactants. The carbon dioxide diffuses into the leaf from the air. The water comes from the soil, up through the roots and the xylem. The oxygen is a waste product. Some is used in respiration, and the rest diffuses out of the leaf. For the Extended paper, learn the balanced equation too. Six C O two, plus six H two O, gives C six H twelve O six, plus six O two. Six carbons, twelve hydrogens and eighteen oxygens on each side. It balances.

## Scene 05 — chlorophyll

So what does the light actually do? Inside the leaf's cells are chloroplasts. They contain chlorophyll, a green pigment. Chlorophyll absorbs light, and transfers energy from light into energy in chemicals: the chemical energy stored in glucose. Careful with your wording here. Chlorophyll doesn't make energy. Energy can't be made. It transfers it. So use the syllabus word: transfers.

## Scene 06 — uses

So the plant has made glucose. What does it do with it? Some is used straight away in respiration, to release energy. Some is turned into starch, as an energy store. Starch is insoluble, so it doesn't affect osmosis in the cells. Some becomes cellulose, to build cell walls. Some is changed into sucrose, for transport in the phloem to other parts of the plant. And some becomes nectar in flowers, to attract insects for pollination. Respiration, starch, cellulose, sucrose, nectar. Learn all five.

## Scene 07 — minerals

Glucose only contains carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. But to grow, a plant also needs proteins, which are made from amino acids. And amino acids contain nitrogen. So plants absorb nitrate ions from the soil, through their roots. Nitrate ions are needed to make amino acids. Without enough, growth is stunted, and the older leaves turn yellow. Magnesium ions are needed to make chlorophyll. Without enough, the leaves turn yellow between the veins, so less light is absorbed, and there's less photosynthesis.

## Scene 08 — leaf

Most photosynthesis happens in the leaves. And most leaves are broad and thin. Broad gives a large surface area, to absorb more light. Thin means a short distance for carbon dioxide to diffuse to the cells inside. Let's cut one open. This is a cross-section of a leaf from a dicotyledonous plant, like a bean. It's a simplified diagram, so let's go layer by layer. On top, the waxy cuticle. It reduces water loss, and it's transparent, so light passes straight through. Under it, the upper epidermis. Its cells are transparent too, with no chloroplasts, so light reaches the layer below. The palisade mesophyll. Tall cells, tightly packed near the top, and full of chloroplasts. They absorb the most light, so this is where most photosynthesis happens.

## Scene 09 — layers

Below that, the spongy mesophyll. Rounder cells, loosely packed, with air spaces between them. The air spaces let carbon dioxide diffuse to the cells, and give a large surface area for gas exchange. Running through the leaf are vascular bundles. Xylem brings in water and mineral ions. Phloem carries sucrose and amino acids away, to the rest of the plant. On the underside, the lower epidermis, with tiny pores called stomata. Carbon dioxide diffuses in through them, and oxygen diffuses out. Each stoma is opened and closed by a pair of guard cells.

## Scene 10 — starch-test

So how do you prove a leaf has been photosynthesising? Test it for starch. First, destarch the plant. Leave it in the dark for a day or two, so it uses up its stored starch. Then any starch you find later must have been made during your experiment. Step one: put the leaf in boiling water. This kills it, and breaks down the cell membranes so the iodine can get in. Step two: turn off the Bunsen burner, and put the leaf in hot ethanol, in a water bath. Ethanol is flammable, so no naked flame. The ethanol removes the chlorophyll, so you can see the colour change. Step three: dip it in warm water to soften it. Step four: spread it on a white tile, and add iodine solution. Blue-black means starch is present. Orange-brown means no starch.

## Scene 11 — needs

Now use that test to show what photosynthesis needs. Each experiment needs a control: identical, except for the one factor you're testing. Chlorophyll: use a variegated leaf, one that's partly green and partly white. The white parts have no chlorophyll, so after the test, only the green parts turn blue-black. Light: cover part of a leaf with black paper, and leave the plant in the light for a few hours. Only the uncovered part makes starch. The uncovered part is the control. Carbon dioxide: seal a leaf in a flask with soda lime, which absorbs carbon dioxide. The control is an identical flask with no soda lime. Only the control leaf turns blue-black. So photosynthesis needs chlorophyll, light, and carbon dioxide.

## Scene 12 — rate

To measure the rate of photosynthesis, use pondweed. Put it in water with a little sodium hydrogencarbonate, which supplies carbon dioxide. In the light, it gives off bubbles of oxygen. Count the bubbles per minute. Or, for more accurate results, collect the gas and measure its volume, because bubbles vary in size. To change the light intensity, move a lamp closer or further away. Keep everything else constant: the same plant, the same carbon dioxide concentration, and the same temperature. An LED lamp helps, because it gives off very little heat. As light intensity increases, the rate increases, then levels off. Increase the carbon dioxide concentration, and you get the same shape: a rise, then a plateau. Temperature is different. The rate rises to an optimum, then falls quickly. The reactions of photosynthesis are catalysed by enzymes. Below the optimum, more heat means more kinetic energy, and more collisions. Above it, the enzymes are denatured.

## Scene 13 — limiting

Extended now: limiting factors. A limiting factor is something in short supply that restricts the rate. Look at the light graph again. Here, the rate rises as light intensity rises, so light intensity is the limiting factor. Here, more light makes no difference. Light is no longer limiting. Something else is, like carbon dioxide concentration, or temperature. Raise the carbon dioxide, and the curve levels off higher. That shows carbon dioxide was limiting. In the exam, name the limiting factor, and give the reason: increasing it increases the rate. Different conditions, different limits. At dawn, or on a dull day, it's usually light. On a cold winter's day, it's often temperature. On a bright, warm summer's day, it's often carbon dioxide.

## Scene 14 — predict

One more practical, for the Extended paper. Hydrogencarbonate indicator shows how much carbon dioxide is in a solution. At normal air levels, it's red. More carbon dioxide, and it turns yellow. Less carbon dioxide, and it turns purple. Three tubes of indicator. One with a leaf, in bright light. One with a leaf, wrapped in foil, so it's dark. And one with no leaf at all. What colour will each tube be, after a few hours? Pause the video, and make your prediction. **(pause 4)**

## Scene 15 — reveal

Here's what happens. In the light, the indicator turns purple. Photosynthesis is faster than respiration, so the leaf takes in more carbon dioxide than it gives out. In the dark, it turns yellow. There's no photosynthesis, but respiration carries on, so carbon dioxide is released. The tube with no leaf stays red. It's the control: it shows the leaf caused the changes. And in dim light, the two rates can balance, so the indicator stays red. Which busts a classic myth: that plants photosynthesise instead of respiring. Plants respire all the time, day and night, just like you. In bright light, photosynthesis is just faster, so there's a net uptake of carbon dioxide. Net is the word examiners look for.

## Scene 16 — resolve

So, back to van Helmont's willow. Where did seventy-four kilograms come from? Carbon dioxide, from the air, and water, from the soil. Built into glucose, then into cellulose, starch and proteins. The soil itself gave only a few grams of mineral ions, like nitrate and magnesium. Van Helmont thought his tree was made of water. He was only half right. The rest was built out of thin air, using energy from light.

## Scene 17 — recap

Quick recap. Carbon dioxide plus water gives glucose plus oxygen, using energy from light. Chlorophyll transfers that energy. Glucose is used in respiration, or becomes starch, cellulose, sucrose or nectar. Nitrate for amino acids. Magnesium for chlorophyll. Leaves are broad, thin, and built in layers. And light, carbon dioxide and temperature can all be limiting factors. That's plant nutrition.
