## S01 hook

{pot} Almost four hundred years ago, a scientist called Jan van Helmont planted a small willow tree in a pot of dried soil. {weigh} He weighed the tree. He weighed the soil. {water} Then, for five years, he gave it nothing but water. [pause 0.6] {grown} The tree grew. It gained about seventy-four kilograms. {soil} And the soil? It had lost only about fifty-seven grams. [pause 0.8] {question} So where did a whole tree's worth of mass come from? [pause 0.5] {notSoil} Not the soil. {air} Most of the wood came out of thin air. ## S03 definition

[pause 2.2] {eat} You eat to get your food. Plants don't. {make} Plants make their own food, by photosynthesis. [pause 0.5] {define} Here's the definition the mark scheme wants. Learn it word for word. [pause 0.3] {defLine} Photosynthesis is the process by which plants synthesise carbohydrates from raw materials using energy from light. [pause 1] {synthesise} Let's break that down. Synthesise means make. {carbs} The carbohydrate it makes is glucose, a simple sugar. {raw} The raw materials are carbon dioxide and water. {light} And the energy to do it comes from light. [pause 0.6]

## S04 equation

{equation} Now the word equation. {co2} Carbon dioxide, {water} plus water, {glucose} gives glucose, {oxygen} plus oxygen. [pause 0.4] {conditions} And it happens in the presence of light and chlorophyll, so we write those on the arrow. {notReactants} They're conditions, not reactants. Light isn't a substance, and chlorophyll isn't used up. [pause 0.6] {inputs} So where do the raw materials come from? {co2From} Carbon dioxide diffuses into the leaf from the air. {waterFrom} Water is absorbed from the soil by the roots, and travels up the stem, in the xylem, to the leaf. [pause 0.4] {oxygenOut} Oxygen is a waste product. Some is used in the plant's own respiration, {release} and the rest diffuses out of the leaf, into the air. [pause 0.6]

## S05 balanced

{symbol} You also need to know the balanced symbol equation. [pause 0.3] {balanced} Six C O two, plus six H two O, gives C six H twelve O six, plus six O two. [pause 0.6] {check} Let's check it balances. {carbon} Carbon: six on the left, six on the right. {hydrogen} Hydrogen: twelve on each side. {oxygenCount} Oxygen: twelve plus six makes eighteen on the left, and six plus twelve makes eighteen on the right. {balances} It balances. [pause 0.6]

## S06 chlorophyll

{what} So, what does the light actually do? [pause 0.4] {chloroplasts} Inside the cells of a leaf are chloroplasts. {chlorophyll} Chloroplasts contain chlorophyll, a green pigment. [pause 0.3] {absorb} Chlorophyll absorbs light. {transfer} Then it transfers energy from light into energy in chemicals, for the synthesis of carbohydrates. {stored} In other words, that energy ends up stored in glucose. [pause 0.6] {careful} Be careful with your wording here. {notMake} Never write that chlorophyll makes energy. Energy can't be made. {transfers} Chlorophyll transfers it. So use the syllabus word: transfers. [pause 0.6]

## S07 uses

[pause 2.2] {uses} So the plant has made glucose. What happens to it? There are five uses to learn. [pause 0.4] {respiration} One. Glucose is used in respiration, to release energy for the plant's cells. {starch} Two. Glucose is turned into starch, as an energy store. {insoluble} Starch is insoluble, so storing it doesn't affect the movement of water by osmosis. {cellulose} Three. Glucose is used to make cellulose, which builds the cell walls. {sucrose} Four. Glucose is changed into sucrose, another sugar. Sucrose is transported in the phloem, to other parts of the plant. {nectar} And five. Sugars are used to make nectar, in flowers, to attract insects for pollination. [pause 0.6] {five} Respiration. Starch. Cellulose. Sucrose. Nectar. [pause 0.6]

## S08 minerals

[pause 2.2] {chon} But glucose only contains carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. {protein} To grow, a plant also needs proteins, and proteins are made from amino acids. {nitrogen} Amino acids contain nitrogen. [pause 0.3] {nitrate} So plants absorb nitrate ions from the soil, through their roots. {nitrateUse} Nitrate ions are needed to make amino acids. {nitrateLack} Without enough nitrate, the plant has stunted growth, and its older leaves turn yellow. [pause 0.6] {magnesium} Plants also absorb magnesium ions. {magnesiumUse} Magnesium ions are needed to make chlorophyll. {magLack} Without enough magnesium, the leaves turn yellow between the veins. {lessPs} Less chlorophyll means less light is absorbed, so there's less photosynthesis. [pause 0.6] {examLine} In an exam, link three things: the ion, what it's used to make, and what happens without it. [pause 0.6]

## S09 leaf

[pause 2.2] {leaf} Most photosynthesis happens in the leaves, and leaves are adapted for it. [pause 0.3] {broadThin} Most leaves are broad and thin. {area} Broad means a large surface area, to absorb more light. {thin} Thin means a short distance for carbon dioxide to diffuse to the cells inside. [pause 0.6] {slice} Now let's cut a leaf open. {section} This is a cross-section through the leaf of a dicotyledonous plant. {dicot} That's a plant whose seeds have two seed leaves. {simplified} It's a simplified diagram. [pause 0.3] {nameExplain} For each part, you need to know its name, and how it helps photosynthesis. {layers} Let's go through it layer by layer, from the top. [pause 0.6] {cuticle} First, the waxy cuticle. {cuticleWhy} It reduces water loss, and because it's transparent, light passes straight through it. [pause 0.3] {upper} Next, the upper epidermis. {upperWhy} These cells are transparent too, and they have no chloroplasts, so light passes through to the cells below. [pause 0.3] {palisade} Then the palisade mesophyll. {palisadeWhy} These cells are tall, tightly packed, close to the top surface, and full of chloroplasts. {most} So they absorb the most light. This is where most photosynthesis happens. [pause 0.6]

## S10 layers

{spongy} Below the palisade is the spongy mesophyll. {spongyWhy} Its cells are rounder, and loosely packed, {air} with air spaces between them. {diffuse} The air spaces let carbon dioxide diffuse to the cells, and they give a large surface area for gas exchange. [pause 0.6] {bundle} Running through the leaf are vascular bundles. {xylem} The xylem brings water and mineral ions into the leaf. {phloem} The phloem carries sucrose and amino acids away, to the rest of the plant. [pause 0.6] {lower} On the underside is the lower epidermis, {stomata} with tiny pores called stomata. One pore is called a stoma. {gases} Carbon dioxide diffuses in through the stomata, and oxygen diffuses out. {guard} Each stoma is opened and closed by a pair of guard cells. {guardOpen} In the light, they open the stomata, so carbon dioxide can diffuse in. [pause 0.6] {recap} So, from top to bottom: cuticle, upper epidermis, palisade mesophyll, spongy mesophyll, vascular bundle, lower epidermis with stomata and guard cells. [pause 0.8]

## S11 starch-test

[pause 2.2] {prove} So how can you show that a leaf has been photosynthesising? {test} Test it for starch, because a leaf stores some of its glucose as starch. [pause 0.6] {destarch} First, destarch the plant. Put it in the dark for a day or two. {useUp} With no light, it can't photosynthesise, so it uses up its stored starch. {why} That means any starch you find later must have been made during your experiment. [pause 0.6] {boil} Step one: put the leaf in boiling water for about a minute. {boilWhy} This kills the cells, and breaks down the cell membranes, so the iodine can get in later. [pause 0.4] {ethanol} Step two: turn off the Bunsen burner. Then put the leaf in a boiling tube of ethanol, and stand it in the hot water. {flammable} Ethanol is flammable, so never heat it with a naked flame. {decolour} The hot ethanol removes the chlorophyll, so the colour change will be easy to see. [pause 0.4] {rinse} Step three: dip the leaf in warm water. {rinseWhy} The ethanol makes it brittle, and the water softens it. [pause 0.4] {tile} Step four: spread the leaf on a white tile, so the colour is easy to see, and add iodine solution. [pause 0.4] {blueBlack} If it turns blue-black, starch is present. {orange} If it stays orange-brown, there's no starch. [pause 0.8]

## S12 needs

[pause 2.2] {need} Now you can use that test to show what photosynthesis needs. {control} Each experiment needs a control. A control is identical to the test, except for the one factor you're investigating. {controlWhy} It shows that the result was caused by that factor. [pause 0.6] {chlorophyllTest} First, chlorophyll. Take a destarched plant with variegated leaves: leaves that are partly green and partly white. {white} The white parts have no chlorophyll. {lightUp} Leave it in the light for a few hours, then test a leaf. {greenOnly} Only the green parts turn blue-black. The white parts stay orange-brown. {chlorophyllControl} The green parts are the control. {chlorophyllNeeded} So chlorophyll is needed. [pause 0.6] {cover} Second, light. Take a destarched plant, and cover part of one leaf with black paper. {sunlit} Leave it in the light for a few hours. {uncovered} After the test, only the uncovered part turns blue-black. {lightControl} The uncovered part is the control. So light is needed. [pause 0.6] {flask} Third, carbon dioxide. Take a destarched plant, and seal one leaf, still attached, inside a flask with soda lime, {absorbs} which absorbs carbon dioxide. {co2Control} The control is an identical flask, with no soda lime. {co2Result} After a few hours in the light, only the control leaf turns blue-black. {co2Needed} So carbon dioxide is needed. [pause 0.8]

## S13 rate-method

[pause 2.2] {rate} Next: how fast is photosynthesis happening? That's its rate. {pondweed} To measure it, use pondweed, a water plant. {setup} Put it in water with a little sodium hydrogencarbonate, which supplies carbon dioxide. {bubbles} In the light, the pondweed gives off bubbles of gas, mostly oxygen. [pause 0.4] {count} Count the bubbles per minute. {syringe} Or, for more accurate results, collect the gas and measure its volume, because bubbles can be different sizes. [pause 0.6] {lamp} To change the light intensity, move a lamp closer or further away. The closer the lamp, the higher the light intensity. {variables} So, light intensity is the independent variable: the one you change. {dependent} The rate is the dependent variable: the one you measure. {constant} And everything else must be kept constant: the same plant, the same carbon dioxide concentration, the same temperature, and no other lights on. {waterBath} To keep the temperature steady, stand the beaker in a water bath. {led} An LED lamp helps, because it gives off much less heat than a filament bulb. [pause 0.4] {acclimatise} At each new distance, wait a few minutes before you count, so the plant can adjust. {repeat} Then repeat, and calculate a mean. [pause 0.6] {otherFactors} You can test the other two factors the same way. Just change one thing at a time. {co2Method} To vary carbon dioxide, use different concentrations of sodium hydrogencarbonate. {tempMethod} To vary temperature, set the water bath to different temperatures. [pause 0.8]

## S14 rate-results

{lightGraph} Here are the results. As light intensity increases, the rate of photosynthesis increases. {levels} Then it levels off. [pause 0.4] {co2Graph} Increase the carbon dioxide concentration, and you get the same shape: the rate increases, then levels off. [pause 0.4] {tempGraph} Temperature is different. The rate increases up to an optimum, then falls quickly. {enzymes} That's because the reactions of photosynthesis are catalysed by enzymes. {below} Below the optimum, raising the temperature gives the molecules more kinetic energy, so there are more frequent effective collisions between enzymes and their substrates. {above} Above the optimum, the enzymes are denatured. Their active sites change shape, so the substrates no longer fit. [pause 0.6] {describe} When a question says describe a graph, say what happens, and quote numbers from the axes, with their units. {explain} When it says explain, say why. [pause 0.8]

## S15 limiting

[pause 2.2] {explainShapes} Now let's explain those shapes, using limiting factors. [pause 0.3] {defn} A limiting factor is something in short supply that limits the rate of a process. [pause 0.6] {graph} Back to the light graph. {rising} On this rising part, the rate increases as light intensity increases. So light intensity is the limiting factor. {plateau} On this flat part, more light makes no difference. Light intensity is no longer the limiting factor. {other} Something else is, like carbon dioxide concentration, or temperature. [pause 0.6] {raise} Now repeat the experiment at a higher carbon dioxide concentration. {same} At low light intensity, the two curves are the same, because light intensity is still the limiting factor. {higher} But the new curve keeps rising for longer, and levels off higher. {proves} That shows carbon dioxide was the limiting factor on the flat part. [pause 0.6] {exam} In the exam, name the limiting factor, and give the reason: increasing it increases the rate. [pause 0.6] {conditions} Different conditions have different limiting factors. {dawn} At dawn, or on a dull day, light intensity is usually the limiting factor, because there's not much light. {winter} On a cold winter's day, it's often temperature. {winterWhy} The molecules have less kinetic energy, so there are fewer effective collisions. {summer} And on a bright, warm summer's day, there's plenty of light and warmth, so it's often carbon dioxide concentration. {summerWhy} There's only a tiny amount of carbon dioxide in the air. [pause 0.8]

## S16 predict

[pause 2.2] {indicator} One more practical. [pause 0.3] {indicatorDef} Hydrogencarbonate indicator shows how much carbon dioxide is in a solution. {red} At the normal level of carbon dioxide in air, it's red. {yellow} With more carbon dioxide, it turns yellow. {purple} With less carbon dioxide, it turns purple. [pause 0.6] {tubes} Here are three tubes of indicator. {bungs} They're sealed with bungs, so no carbon dioxide from the air can get in or out. {tubeA} One has pondweed, in bright light. {tubeB} One has pondweed too, but its tube is wrapped in foil, so it's dark inside. {tubeC} And one has no pondweed at all. [pause 0.3] {question} What colour will each tube be, after a few hours? [pause 0.6] {pauseNow} Pause the video, and make your prediction. [pause 4]

## S17 reveal

{reveal} Here's what happens. [pause 0.3] {lightTube} In the light, the indicator turns purple. Photosynthesis is faster than respiration, so the pondweed takes in more carbon dioxide than it gives out. [pause 0.4] {darkTube} In the dark, it turns yellow. There's no photosynthesis, but respiration carries on, so the pondweed gives out carbon dioxide. [pause 0.4] {controlTube} The tube with no pondweed stays red. That's the control. It shows that the pondweed caused the colour changes. [pause 0.4] {dim} And in dim light, the two rates can be equal. {noNet} Then there's no net uptake or release of carbon dioxide, so the indicator stays red. [pause 0.6] {myth} Which busts a classic myth: that plants only respire at night. {bust} Plants respire all the time, day and night, just like you. {net} In bright light, photosynthesis is just faster, so there's a net uptake of carbon dioxide. {netWord} Net means overall: the plant takes in more carbon dioxide than it gives out. {netExam} Net is the word examiners look for. [pause 0.8]

## S18 resolve

{back} So, back to van Helmont's willow. {where} Where did those seventy-four kilograms come from? [pause 0.5] {co2In} Carbon dioxide, from the air, {waterIn} and water, from the soil. {built} Joined into glucose, using energy from light, then built into cellulose, starch and proteins. [pause 0.4] {minerals} The little that the soil lost was mostly mineral ions, like nitrate and magnesium. [pause 0.5] {thought} Van Helmont thought his tree was made of water. {partly} He was only partly right. A living tree is full of water. {wood} But most of the wood itself was built from carbon dioxide, {thinAir} taken out of thin air. [pause 0.8]

## S19 recap

{recap} Let's recap. [pause 0.3] {r1} Photosynthesis: carbon dioxide plus water gives glucose plus oxygen, in the presence of light and chlorophyll. {r2} Chlorophyll transfers energy from light into energy in chemicals. {r3} Glucose is used for respiration, starch, cellulose, sucrose and nectar. {r4} Nitrate ions are needed to make amino acids. Magnesium ions are needed to make chlorophyll. {r5} Leaves are broad, thin, and built in layers, each adapted for photosynthesis. {r6} Light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration and temperature can all be limiting factors. [pause 0.6] {title} And that's plant nutrition.
