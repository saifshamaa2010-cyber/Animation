# EP002 — Plant Nutrition: How a Tree Is Built from Thin Air (working title)

## S01 hook

{pot} Almost four hundred years ago, a scientist called Jan van Helmont planted a small willow tree in a pot of dry soil. {weigh} He weighed the tree. He weighed the soil. {water} Then, for five years, he gave it nothing but water. [pause 0.4] {grown} The tree grew. It gained about seventy-four kilograms. {soil} And the soil? It had lost about fifty-seven grams. [pause 0.5] {question} So where did a whole tree's worth of mass come from? [pause 0.3] {notSoil} Not the soil. {air} Most of it came out of thin air.

## S03 definition

{eat} You eat to get your food. Plants don't. {make} They make their own, by photosynthesis. [pause 0.3] {define} Here's the definition the mark scheme wants. {defLine} Photosynthesis is the process by which plants synthesise carbohydrates from raw materials using energy from light. [pause 0.4] {synthesise} Synthesise just means make. {carbs} The carbohydrate they make first is glucose, a sugar. {raw} The raw materials are carbon dioxide and water. {light} And the energy comes from light.

## S04 equation

{equation} Here's the word equation. {co2} Carbon dioxide, {water} plus water, {glucose} gives glucose, {oxygen} plus oxygen. {conditions} Light and chlorophyll go above the arrow. {notReactants} They're conditions, not reactants. [pause 0.3] {co2From} The carbon dioxide diffuses into the leaf from the air. {waterFrom} The water comes from the soil, up through the roots and the xylem. {oxygenOut} The oxygen is a waste product. Some is used in respiration, and the rest diffuses out of the leaf. [pause 0.4] {extended} For the Extended paper, learn the balanced equation too. {balanced} Six C O two, plus six H two O, gives C six H twelve O six, plus six O two. {count} Six carbons, twelve hydrogens and eighteen oxygens on each side. It balances.

## S05 chlorophyll

{what} So what does the light actually do? [pause 0.2] {chloroplasts} Inside the leaf's cells are chloroplasts. {chlorophyll} They contain chlorophyll, a green pigment. {absorb} Chlorophyll absorbs light, {transfer} and transfers energy from light into energy in chemicals: {stored} the chemical energy stored in glucose. [pause 0.3] {careful} Careful with your wording here. {notMake} Chlorophyll doesn't make energy. Energy can't be made. {transfers} It transfers it. So use the syllabus word: transfers.

## S06 uses

{uses} So the plant has made glucose. What does it do with it? [pause 0.3] {respiration} Some is used straight away in respiration, to release energy. {starch} Some is turned into starch, as an energy store. {insoluble} Starch is insoluble, so it doesn't affect osmosis in the cells. {cellulose} Some becomes cellulose, to build cell walls. {sucrose} Some is changed into sucrose, for transport in the phloem to other parts of the plant. {nectar} And some becomes nectar in flowers, to attract insects for pollination. [pause 0.3] {five} Respiration, starch, cellulose, sucrose, nectar. Learn all five.

## S07 minerals

{chon} Glucose only contains carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. {protein} But to grow, a plant also needs proteins, which are made from amino acids. {nitrogen} And amino acids contain nitrogen. {nitrate} So plants absorb nitrate ions from the soil, through their roots. {nitrateUse} Nitrate ions are needed to make amino acids. {nitrateLack} Without enough, growth is stunted, and the older leaves turn yellow. [pause 0.3] {magnesium} Magnesium ions are needed to make chlorophyll. {magLack} Without enough, the leaves turn yellow between the veins, {lessPs} so less light is absorbed, and there's less photosynthesis.

## S08 leaf

{leaf} Most photosynthesis happens in the leaves. {broadThin} And most leaves are broad and thin. {area} Broad gives a large surface area, to absorb more light. {thin} Thin means a short distance for carbon dioxide to diffuse to the cells inside. [pause 0.4] {slice} Let's cut one open. {section} This is a cross-section of a leaf from a dicotyledonous plant, like a bean. {simplified} It's a simplified diagram, so let's go layer by layer. [pause 0.2] {cuticle} On top, the waxy cuticle. It reduces water loss, {clear} and it's transparent, so light passes straight through. {upper} Under it, the upper epidermis. Its cells are transparent too, with no chloroplasts, so light reaches the layer below. {palisade} The palisade mesophyll. Tall cells, tightly packed near the top, and full of chloroplasts. {most} They absorb the most light, so this is where most photosynthesis happens.

## S09 layers

{spongy} Below that, the spongy mesophyll. Rounder cells, loosely packed, {air} with air spaces between them. {diffuse} The air spaces let carbon dioxide diffuse to the cells, and give a large surface area for gas exchange. [pause 0.3] {bundle} Running through the leaf are vascular bundles. {xylem} Xylem brings in water and mineral ions. {phloem} Phloem carries sucrose and amino acids away, to the rest of the plant. [pause 0.3] {lower} On the underside, the lower epidermis, {stomata} with tiny pores called stomata. {gases} Carbon dioxide diffuses in through them, and oxygen diffuses out. {guard} Each stoma is opened and closed by a pair of guard cells.

## S10 starch-test

{prove} So how do you prove a leaf has been photosynthesising? {test} Test it for starch. [pause 0.3] {destarch} First, destarch the plant. Leave it in the dark for a day or two, {useUp} so it uses up its stored starch. {why} Then any starch you find later must have been made during your experiment. [pause 0.3] {boil} Step one: put the leaf in boiling water. This kills it, and breaks down the cell membranes so the iodine can get in. {ethanol} Step two: turn off the Bunsen burner, and put the leaf in hot ethanol, in a water bath. {flammable} Ethanol is flammable, so no naked flame. {decolour} The ethanol removes the chlorophyll, so you can see the colour change. {rinse} Step three: dip it in warm water to soften it. {tile} Step four: spread it on a white tile, and add iodine solution. {blueBlack} Blue-black means starch is present. {orange} Orange-brown means no starch.

## S11 needs

{need} Now use that test to show what photosynthesis needs. {control} Each experiment needs a control: identical, except for the one factor you're testing. [pause 0.3] {variegated} Chlorophyll: use a variegated leaf, one that's partly green and partly white. {white} The white parts have no chlorophyll, {greenOnly} so after the test, only the green parts turn blue-black. [pause 0.3] {cover} Light: cover part of a leaf with black paper, {sunlit} and leave the plant in the light for a few hours. {uncovered} Only the uncovered part makes starch. {lightControl} The uncovered part is the control. [pause 0.3] {flask} Carbon dioxide: seal a leaf in a flask with soda lime, {absorbs} which absorbs carbon dioxide. {co2Control} The control is an identical flask with no soda lime. {co2Result} Only the control leaf turns blue-black. [pause 0.3] {needs} So photosynthesis needs chlorophyll, light, and carbon dioxide.

## S12 rate

{rate} To measure the rate of photosynthesis, use pondweed. {setup} Put it in water with a little sodium hydrogencarbonate, which supplies carbon dioxide. {bubbles} In the light, it gives off bubbles of oxygen. {count} Count the bubbles per minute. {syringe} Or, for more accurate results, collect the gas and measure its volume, because bubbles vary in size. [pause 0.3] {lamp} To change the light intensity, move a lamp closer or further away. {constant} Keep everything else constant: the same plant, the same carbon dioxide concentration, and the same temperature. {led} An LED lamp helps, because it gives off very little heat. [pause 0.3] {lightGraph} As light intensity increases, the rate increases, then levels off. {co2Graph} Increase the carbon dioxide concentration, and you get the same shape: a rise, then a plateau. {tempGraph} Temperature is different. The rate rises to an optimum, then falls quickly. {enzymes} The reactions of photosynthesis are catalysed by enzymes. {below} Below the optimum, more heat means more kinetic energy, and more collisions. {above} Above it, the enzymes are denatured.

## S13 limiting

{extended} Extended now: limiting factors. {defn} A limiting factor is something in short supply that restricts the rate. [pause 0.3] {graph} Look at the light graph again. {rising} Here, the rate rises as light intensity rises, so light intensity is the limiting factor. {plateau} Here, more light makes no difference. Light is no longer limiting. {other} Something else is, like carbon dioxide concentration, or temperature. {raise} Raise the carbon dioxide, and the curve levels off higher. {proves} That shows carbon dioxide was limiting. [pause 0.3] {exam} In the exam, name the limiting factor, and give the reason: increasing it increases the rate. [pause 0.3] {dawn} Different conditions, different limits. At dawn, or on a dull day, it's usually light. {winter} On a cold winter's day, it's often temperature. {summer} On a bright, warm summer's day, it's often carbon dioxide.

## S14 predict

{indicator} One more practical, for the Extended paper. Hydrogencarbonate indicator shows how much carbon dioxide is in a solution. {red} At normal air levels, it's red. {yellow} More carbon dioxide, and it turns yellow. {purple} Less carbon dioxide, and it turns purple. [pause 0.3] {tubes} Three tubes of indicator. {tubeA} One with a leaf, in bright light. {tubeB} One with a leaf, wrapped in foil, so it's dark. {tubeC} And one with no leaf at all. {question} What colour will each tube be, after a few hours? [pause 0.6] {pauseNow} Pause the video, and make your prediction. [pause 4]

## S15 reveal

{reveal} Here's what happens. {lightTube} In the light, the indicator turns purple. Photosynthesis is faster than respiration, so the leaf takes in more carbon dioxide than it gives out. {darkTube} In the dark, it turns yellow. There's no photosynthesis, but respiration carries on, so carbon dioxide is released. {controlTube} The tube with no leaf stays red. It's the control: it shows the leaf caused the changes. {dim} And in dim light, the two rates can balance, so the indicator stays red. [pause 0.4] {myth} Which busts a classic myth: that plants photosynthesise instead of respiring. {bust} Plants respire all the time, day and night, just like you. {net} In bright light, photosynthesis is just faster, so there's a net uptake of carbon dioxide. {netWord} Net is the word examiners look for.

## S16 resolve

{back} So, back to van Helmont's willow. {where} Where did seventy-four kilograms come from? [pause 0.3] {co2In} Carbon dioxide, from the air, {waterIn} and water, from the soil. {built} Built into glucose, then into cellulose, starch and proteins. {minerals} The soil itself gave only a few grams of mineral ions, like nitrate and magnesium. [pause 0.3] {thought} Van Helmont thought his tree was made of water. {half} He was only half right. [pause 0.4] {thinAir} The rest was built out of thin air, using energy from light.

## S17 recap

{recap} Quick recap. {r1} Carbon dioxide plus water gives glucose plus oxygen, using energy from light. {r2} Chlorophyll transfers that energy. {r3} Glucose is used in respiration, or becomes starch, cellulose, sucrose or nectar. {r4} Nitrate for amino acids. Magnesium for chlorophyll. {r5} Leaves are broad, thin, and built in layers. {r6} And light, carbon dioxide and temperature can all be limiting factors. [pause 0.5] {title} That's plant nutrition.
