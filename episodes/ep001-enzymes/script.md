## S03 starch

{starch} Crackers are packed with starch. {zoom} Zoom right in, and starch is a chain: {chain} hundreds, even thousands, of glucose molecules, linked end to end. {notSweet} Glucose is a sugar, but locked up in a chain like this, it doesn't taste sweet. [pause 0.3] {links} And those links are tough. {aeons} Left alone in water, a single link would take millions of years to break.

## S04 enzyme

[pause 0.3] {amylase} Enter amylase: an enzyme in your saliva. {snip} It snips starch into smaller sugars, {maltose} like maltose, which is just two glucose units joined together. {sweetTaste} And maltose does taste sweet. [pause 0.4] {catalyst} That's what an enzyme is: a biological catalyst. It speeds up a reaction {unchanged} without being used up. {protein} Nearly all enzymes are proteins, long chains of amino acids {fold} folded into a precise shape. {shape} And that shape is everything.

## S05 active-site

[pause 0.3] {site} Every enzyme has a small pocket called the active site. {substrate} The molecule it works on, the substrate, fits inside, {complex} forming an enzyme–substrate complex. {held} Held there, a bond becomes much easier to break. {break} Snap. {release} The products leave, {next} and the active site is free for the next one. [pause 0.4] {lockKey} The shapes have to match, like a lock and a key. {specific} That's why enzymes are so specific. {cellulose} Amylase can't even break down cellulose, in paper, though that's a chain of glucose too. {differentLink} Its links are joined differently, so it just doesn't fit.

## S06 induced-fit

[pause 0.3] {simplification} Lock and key is a brilliant model, but it is a simplification. {flex} Real enzymes aren't rigid. {grip} As the substrate binds, the active site moulds itself around it. {inducedFit} That more accurate picture is called induced fit. {exam} For GCSE, lock and key is the one to use. Just remember: it's a model, not a photograph.

## S07 myth-used-up

[pause 0.4] {myth1} Which brings us to myth number one: that enzymes get used up. {bust1} They don't. After each reaction, the enzyme is unchanged, {again} ready to go again, and again. {catalase} And some are astonishingly fast. One molecule of catalase, from your liver, can break down millions of molecules of hydrogen peroxide a second. [pause 0.3] {wear} Enzymes don't last forever: your cells replace old ones. {notConsumed} But the reaction itself never uses them up.

## S08 temperature

[pause 0.4] {temperature} Now, temperature. {jiggle} Molecules are always jiggling around. {warm} Heat them up, and they move faster. {collide} Enzymes and substrates collide more often, with more energy, {faster} so more reactions happen. {upToAPoint} Up to a point, warmer means faster. {optimum} For many enzymes in your body, that peak, the optimum, is around 37 degrees: body temperature.

## S09 predict

[pause 0.4] {predict} So what if we keep turning up the heat? {t50} Fifty degrees. {t60} Sixty. {t70} Seventy. {question} Does it just keep getting faster? [pause 0.6] {pauseNow} Pause the video, and make your prediction. [pause 4]

## S10 denature

{reveal} Here's what actually happens. {crash} The rate crashes, and it doesn't recover. [pause 0.3] {bonds} An enzyme's shape is held together by lots of weak bonds. {shake} Too much heat, and it vibrates so violently that those bonds break. {unravel} The chain unravels, {siteLost} the active site loses its shape, {noFit} and the substrate no longer fits. {denatured} The enzyme has been denatured. [pause 0.3] {egg} It's the same kind of change that turns egg white solid in a hot pan, {noUndo} and, like a cooked egg, cooling it down usually doesn't undo it. [pause 0.3] {cold} Cold is different: it just slows enzymes down. {rewarm} Warm them up, and they recover.

## S11 myth-killed

[pause 0.4] {myth2} And that's myth number two: that heat "kills" enzymes. {notAlive} Enzymes were never alive. {molecule} An enzyme is a molecule, a very clever one, but it can't die. {correct} It's denatured: its active site changes shape, so the substrate no longer fits. {marks} In an exam, that's the answer that gets the marks.

## S12 ph

[pause 0.4] {ph} pH can do this too. {disrupt} Too acidic, or too alkaline, and the bonds holding an enzyme's shape are disrupted. {phOptimum} So every enzyme has an optimum pH. {amylasePh} Amylase, in your mouth, works best close to neutral, around pH 7. {stomach} Your stomach is strongly acidic, often around pH 2, {pepsin} and pepsin, the enzyme that digests protein there, works best right around that. {tuned} Different enzymes, tuned to different places.

## S13 resolve

[pause 0.5] {back} So, back to that cracker. {chew} As you chew, amylase meets the starch. {fit} The chains fit its active site, {cut} and it snips them into maltose, {again} over and over, without being used up. {conditions} Your mouth is about 37 degrees and close to neutral: just right. {aeons} Links that would last millions of years on their own are broken while you chew. {tongue} That sweetness is enzymes at work, in real time. [pause 0.6] {title} And that's how enzymes actually work.
