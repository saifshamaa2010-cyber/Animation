## S03 starch

{starch} Crackers are packed with starch. {zoom} Zoom right in, and starch turns out to be a chain. {chain} Hundreds, even thousands, of glucose molecules, linked end to end. {notSweet} Glucose is a sugar, but locked up in a chain like this, it doesn't taste sweet. [pause 0.3] {links} And those links are tough. {aeons} Leave one in water at room temperature, and on average it would last millions of years.

## S04 enzyme

[pause 0.3] {amylase} Enter amylase: an enzyme made in your salivary glands. It's a carbohydrase: {snip} it snips starch into smaller sugars, {maltose} like maltose, which is just two glucose units joined together. {sweetTaste} And maltose does taste sweet. [pause 0.4] {catalyst} That's what an enzyme is: a biological catalyst. It speeds up a reaction {unchanged} without being used up, and it comes out unchanged. {everywhere} Nearly every reaction in your body has one, whether it's building things up or breaking them down. [pause 0.2] Without enzymes, your metabolism would be far too slow to keep you alive. {protein} Nearly all enzymes are proteins: long chains of amino acids, {fold} folded into a precise shape. {shape} And that shape is everything.

## S05 active-site

[pause 0.3] {site} Every enzyme has a small pocket called the active site. {substrate} The molecule it works on, the substrate, fits inside, {complex} forming an enzyme–substrate complex. {held} Held there, a bond becomes much easier to break. {break} Snap. {release} The products leave, {next} and the active site is free for the next one. [pause 0.4] {lockKey} The active site is complementary to the substrate: not the same shape, but a perfect fit. The enzyme is the lock. The substrate is the key. [pause 0.2] {specific} That's why enzymes are specific. Each one only fits its own substrate. {cellulose} Amylase can't even digest paper. It's mostly cellulose, another chain of glucose, {differentLink} but its links are joined differently, so it just doesn't fit.

## S06 induced-fit

[pause 0.3] {simplification} Lock and key is a brilliant model, but it's a simplification. {flex} Real enzymes aren't rigid. {grip} As the substrate binds, the active site moulds itself around it. {inducedFit} That more accurate picture is called induced fit. {exam} For GCSE and IGCSE, lock and key is all you need. Induced fit is an A-level idea. Just remember: it's a model, not a photograph. [pause 0.6]

## S07 myth-used-up

[pause 0.4] {myth1} Which brings us to myth number one: that enzymes get used up. {bust1} They don't. After each reaction, the enzyme is unchanged, {again} ready to go again, and again. {catalase} And some are astonishingly fast. Catalase is found in almost all your cells. One molecule of it can break down hundreds of thousands of molecules of hydrogen peroxide. Every second. [pause 0.3] {wear} Your cells do replace old enzymes, eventually. {notConsumed} But the reaction itself never uses them up.

## S08 temperature

[pause 0.4] {temperature} Now, temperature. {jiggle} Molecules are always jiggling around. {warm} Heat them up, and they gain kinetic energy. They move faster. {collide} Enzymes and substrates bump into each other more often, and with more energy. [pause 0.3] {faster} So more collisions are successful, with the substrate landing in the active site. More enzyme–substrate complexes form, and the rate goes up. {upToAPoint} Up to a point, warmer means faster. {optimum} The temperature where the rate peaks is called the optimum. For many enzymes in your body, it's around 37 degrees: body temperature.

## S09 predict

[pause 0.4] {predict} So what if we keep turning up the heat? {t50} Fifty degrees. {t60} Sixty. {t70} Seventy. {question} Does it just keep getting faster? [pause 0.6] {pauseNow} Pause the video, and make your prediction. [pause 4]

## S10 denature

{reveal} Here's what actually happens. {crash} The rate crashes, and it doesn't recover. [pause 0.3] {bonds} An enzyme's shape is held together by lots of weak bonds. {shake} Too much heat, and it vibrates so violently that those bonds break. {unravel} The chain unravels, {siteLost} the active site loses its shape, {noFit} and the substrate no longer fits. {denatured} The enzyme has been denatured. [pause 0.3] {egg} It's the same kind of change that turns egg white solid in a hot pan. {noUndo} You can't uncook an egg, and cooling usually won't fix the enzyme either. [pause 0.3] {cold} Cold is different. Molecules have less kinetic energy, so there are fewer collisions. But the enzyme isn't denatured. {rewarm} Warm it up, and it recovers. [pause 0.6]

## S11 myth-killed

[pause 0.4] {myth2} And that's myth number two: that heat "kills" enzymes. {notAlive} Enzymes were never alive. {molecule} An enzyme is a molecule, a very clever one, but it can't die. {correct} It's denatured. Its active site changes shape, so the substrate no longer fits, and no enzyme–substrate complex can form. {marks} In an exam, that's the answer that gets the marks.

## S12 ph

[pause 0.4] {ph} pH, how acidic or alkaline something is, can denature enzymes too. {disrupt} Too far either way, and the bonds holding the active site in shape are disrupted. [pause 0.3] {phOptimum} So every enzyme has an optimum pH. {amylasePh} Amylase, in your mouth, works best close to neutral, around pH 7. {stomach} Your stomach is strongly acidic, often around pH 2. {pepsin} And pepsin, a protease that digests protein there, works best right around that. {tuned} Different enzymes, tuned to different places.

## S13 resolve

[pause 0.5] {back} So, back to that cracker. {chew} As you chew, amylase meets the starch. {fit} The chains slot into its active site, {cut} and it snips them into maltose, {again} over and over, without being used up. {conditions} Your mouth is about 37 degrees, and close to neutral. Just right. {aeons} Links that would last a million years or more on their own? Broken while you chew. {tongue} That sweet taste is you, tasting enzymes at work. [pause 0.6] {shapeLine} It all comes down to shape. {title} And that's how enzymes actually work.
